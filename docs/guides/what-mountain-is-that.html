What mountain is that? How to name the peaks you can see

You're on a trail or at a lookout, there's a line of peaks on the horizon, and you want to know what they're called. The answer depends only on where you are and which way you're looking. This guide covers the low-tech ways to find out, the digital ones, why it's harder than it looks, and how to prepare so you don't have to guess on the day.

Start with what you know

Before you reach for any tool, note three things: your position (a trailhead, a village, a lake, or GPS coordinates), the direction you're looking (roughly north, east and so on), and a few features of the mountains themselves: a sharp horn, a rounded dome, a snowfield, a notch. You'll use all three to check any answer you get.

Low-tech ways to find out

  • Viewpoint indicators. Many lookouts have an engraved board or dial that names the peaks around you with heights and directions. These are called orientation tables, or toposcopes.
  • Signs and trail guides. Hut walls, trailhead boards and local guidebooks often show a panoramic sketch with names.
  • Ask. Hut wardens, local guides and other walkers usually know.

A map and a compass

  1. Find your position on a topographic map.
  2. Hold the map level and turn it so north on the map points to north on the ground, using the compass.
  3. Point the compass at the mountain and read the bearing.
  4. Draw that line from your position across the map, and look for a summit along it.
  5. Check its shape against what you see: if the map says a long ridge with a notch and you see a pyramid, try a neighbouring peak.

Digital ways to find out

Apps know the terrain from elevation data, plus your location and, sometimes, the direction your phone is pointing. They work in two main ways:

  • Live camera labels. Hold the phone up and names appear over the camera view. It's quick, but the labels rely on the phone's compass, which can be tens of degrees off, so labels may float beside the right peak.
  • A panorama from a point. The app draws the horizon from your position as a 360-degree view with names. You turn it until it looks like what's in front of you. It doesn't depend on the compass, and it works for a place you aren't at yet.

Why it's harder than it looks

  • Distance hides height. A big mountain 100 km away can look smaller than a hill nearby.
  • Foreground blocks the view. The summit you see may be one behind the ridge you thought was the horizon.
  • The curve of the Earth takes the bottom. Far peaks show only their top.
  • Zoom changes the picture. A phone zoom narrows the view, so a peak that looked central is off-centre.

Prepare before you go

If you're planning a hike, look at the view from your destination at home, so you already know what to expect. Pick three landmarks you will recognise, such as a distinctive peak, a lake or a glacier. Download maps or areas for offline use, and charge your phone. Always carry a proper map and check the weather: an app is a guide, not a navigation aid.

Doing it with Toposcope

Live camera labels

  1. Open the Live tab and tap Turn on the camera. Allow the camera and your location. Live mode needs an iPhone with a camera and compass.
  2. Point at the mountains. Toposcope loads the terrain around you (the first time from a new place this is about 10 MB) and puts the peak names over the camera view.
  3. If the labels sit beside the ridge instead of on it, aim at a ridge with sky above and tap Skyline Lock. Toposcope corrects the compass from what the camera sees and tells you how much it moved.

A panorama from anywhere

  1. Open the Panorama tab. Tap the location arrow (My location) to look from where you are, or tap the map button (Choose a viewpoint).
  2. In the map, search for a place or a peak, or move the map so the cross sits on your spot, then tap Show the view from here.
  3. Drag the panorama to look around the full 360°. The caption tells you how many named summits are visible from there.
  4. Tap a peak for its height, distance and direction. I've climbed this adds it to your Summit log, and View from the summit moves the viewpoint to the top.

For a trip without signal

With Toposcope Pro, tap the gear icon on the Photos or Peaks tab, open Offline areas and tap Download an area before you go. Move the map so the cross sits at the centre of your hike and tap Continue, then give the area a name, choose a radius of 15, 30 or 60 km (10, 20 or 40 miles if your units are miles) and tap Download. Panoramas, photos and live labels then work in that area without signal. The four included example photos always work offline.

Peak names and heights come from OpenStreetMap and may contain errors. In the mountains, use a proper map and your own judgement.
