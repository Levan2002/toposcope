How far away can you see a mountain? Horizon distance explained

On a clear day you can see mountains a very long way off. A 4,000 m peak can be in view from over 200 km away if nothing is in the way. How far exactly depends on two heights: where your eyes are and how tall the mountain is. This guide gives you the formula, a table of distances, and the reasons real-world views are shorter than the maths says.

The short answer

The Earth curves away beneath you, so there is a horizon: the farthest ground you can see from where you stand. A mountain can be seen beyond your horizon, because its top sticks up above the curve. The higher you are and the taller the mountain, the farther the sight line reaches.

The formula

For an object of height h above sea level, the distance to its horizon is about:

  • 3.57 × √h kilometres, with h in metres (the geometric distance, ignoring the atmosphere).
  • 1.22 × √h miles, with h in feet.

The atmosphere bends light slightly downward, so in practice you see a little farther. A common correction for normal conditions raises the factor to about 3.86 for kilometres (1.32 for miles in feet), about 8 percent more.

Seeing a mountain from the ground

To see a distant summit from where you stand, add the two horizon distances together: d ≈ 3.57 × (√heye + √hpeak) km. For example, standing on a beach with your eyes 2 m above sea level, a 3,000 m summit comes into line of sight at about 200 km (a little more with refraction). From a 1,000 m ridge looking at the same summit, it's about 3.57 × (31.6 + 54.8) ≈ 310 km.

How much of the mountain is hidden?

Even when you can see the summit, you can't see all of it. The curve of the Earth hides the bottom. The height hidden at a distance d is roughly d² ÷ 12,742 (in kilometres, both). That's about 200 m at 50 km, about 785 m at 100 km, and about 3 km at 200 km. Atmospheric refraction trims those figures by roughly a seventh. This is why a far-off mountain looks like a floating, pale top with the base missing.

Why real views are shorter

  • Haze and humidity. Dust, pollution and moisture wash out far mountains long before the geometry does. The clearest views usually come after rain or a cold front.
  • Terrain in the way. The formula assumes open sea. A nearer ridge can block a taller range behind it. Whether a peak is in line of sight depends on the actual ground between you and it.
  • Light and contrast. Distant ridges fade into layers of paler blue. Low sun behind the mountains, or glare, can wipe them out.
  • Your eyes and your lens. Far summits can be small; binoculars or a long lens help you find them.

How to see farther

  • Go higher. The distance grows with the square root of height, so the first hundred metres give the most.
  • Look on a clear day after a front has passed, and early in the morning before the haze builds.
  • Know where to look. Check the line of sight from your viewpoint ahead of time, and note the direction and distance of the peaks you want.

Doing it with Toposcope

Toposcope works out line of sight for you, using real terrain data, including the curvature of the Earth and atmospheric refraction, out to 220 km.

  1. Open the Panorama tab. Tap My location, or tap Choose a viewpoint, search a place or a peak, and tap Show the view from here.
  2. Drag the panorama to look around. Only summits that are in line of sight over the terrain are drawn and named, and the caption shows how many named summits are visible from there.
  3. Tap a peak to see its height, distance and direction. Tap the gear icon on the Photos or Peaks tab, and under Units and labels choose Metres & kilometres or Feet & miles and turn on Show distances on labels.
  4. Open any peak and tap View from the summit to see how far the view reaches from the top.
  5. For a photo you took, open the Photos tab and tap Choose a photo: each named peak is listed with its distance from where you stood.

Toposcope models the terrain, not the weather, so a peak that's in line of sight may still disappear in haze. Names, heights and terrain can contain errors; in the mountains, use a proper map.
