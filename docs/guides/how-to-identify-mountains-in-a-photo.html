How to identify the mountains in a photo

You took a photo of a beautiful skyline and now you want to know what the peaks are called. It can be done, because a mountain range is the same landscape from every angle. What you need is where the photo was taken and which way the camera pointed. This guide shows how to find both, four ways to name the peaks, and why so many automatic labels end up hanging over empty sky.

Step 1: find out where the photo was taken

Most phone photos save a GPS location in their metadata, as long as Location Services was on for the Camera app. On an iPhone, open the photo in Photos and swipe up to see the details and a small map. If there's no map, the location wasn't saved. This is common with screenshots and with photos that were sent through some messaging apps or downloaded from social media, which often strip location data.

If your photo has no location, work it out from memory: the trailhead, the viewpoint name, the town. A rough position is enough to start, and you can refine it later by looking at the shape of the terrain.

Step 2: work out which way you were facing

Many phones also record the direction the camera was pointing, but that compass reading is often far off (more on that below). If you can, check it against other clues: where the sun was at that time of day, the direction of the valley or trail, a lake or village you can see, or a map. Even "roughly north-east" helps.

Four ways to name the peaks

1. Use a viewpoint indicator

Many lookouts have an engraved dial or board showing the peaks with names, heights and directions. These are sometimes called orientation tables, or toposcopes. They're accurate, but they only exist at certain spots, and you have to be there.

2. A topographic map and a bearing

Mark your position on a map. Draw sight lines in the direction you faced and look along them for tall, distinctive peaks. Compare shapes in your photo with the contour lines: a notch, a glacier, a pyramid-shaped summit. It works and teaches you a lot, but it's slow, and distant peaks are hard to place on a flat map.

3. A panorama tool

Apps and websites can draw the terrain around a point as a labelled skyline. You set your position, look in the right direction, and compare their drawing with your photo by eye. It's quicker than a map, but you do the matching yourself.

4. Automatic photo matching

Some tools draw the terrain from your position and then line it up with the real skyline in your picture automatically, so each label sits on its summit. This needs a good terrain model and a clear skyline, but it's the fastest way when it works.

Why labels are often in the wrong place

Labels from a photo's saved compass heading often miss because a phone compass is easily thrown off. Metal, magnets in cases and mounts, electronics nearby, or a compass that hasn't been calibrated can all push it out by 20 degrees or more. A typical wide-angle phone photo covers only roughly 60 to 70 degrees from left to right, so an error like that can slide every label sideways by a third of the picture. The peaks are right; the direction is wrong.

The way around it is to stop trusting the compass and use the skyline itself. The outline of the ridge against the sky is a fingerprint: if you draw the terrain from the right place and tilt it until its outline fits the one in the photo, you get both the direction and the labels.

Tips for matching by eye

  • Anchor on the most distinctive peak first. A sharp horn, a lone pyramid or a clear notch is easier to find than a rounded ridge.
  • Remember perspective. The peak that looks tallest is often just the closest. A higher mountain far away can look small.
  • Check your zoom. A zoomed photo shows a narrower slice than the lens suggests, and labels need to know that.
  • Look for the foreground. A nearby ridge or trees can hide the base of the mountains behind.
  • Use snow, glaciers and cloud as clues but not as proof, since they change with the season and the day.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up a nearby ridge with the distant range behind it.
  • Assuming the camera pointed where the compass says.
  • Using a photo whose location was removed, then guessing the position.
  • Trusting the first match: check a second peak before you decide.

Doing it with Toposcope

Toposcope does all of this for you, on your iPhone, with no account.

  1. Open the Photos tab and tap Choose a photo. Pick your mountain picture; Toposcope only sees the photos you choose, never the rest of your library.
  2. Toposcope reads the position, the compass heading and the focal length saved in the photo and draws the terrain from that spot, out to 220 km. The first time you look from a new place it downloads about 10 MB of terrain and keeps it.
  3. Skyline Lock then matches the drawing to the real skyline and aligns them. When it works you see "Skyline locked" with a confidence figure, and every visible peak is named, with its height and distance. Peaks are also listed below the photo.
  4. No location? You'll see "This photo has no location". Tap Choose on the map, move the map so the cross sits where you stood, and confirm.
  5. To see the difference, switch between Skyline Lock and Compass only (it appears when the lock moved the heading).
  6. If labels are slightly off, tap Align, drag the photo until the cyan ridge line sits on the real skyline, and pinch to change the field of view. Lock again re-runs the match; Done finishes. Labels lets you change how many names show and turn distances or the terrain skyline on or off. Use the share button to send the labelled photo.

Cloud on the summits, fog, or trees and buildings covering the skyline can stop the match ("Couldn't lock onto the skyline"); in that case align by hand as above. Peak names and heights come from OpenStreetMap and can contain errors, so double-check anything you rely on.
